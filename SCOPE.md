# Scope

What the map covers, what qualifies a site for inclusion, and which settlements
depended on other kinds of water. This is the reference for deciding new
entries; the field-by-field format is in [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md).

## Inclusion criteria

A place belongs on the map if **its water supply, farmland or transport
depended on a named river system**, and it is one of:

- a city or state capital;
- a ceremonial or ritual centre that organised a region;
- a village or site marking a first — earliest farming, earliest rice, earliest
  monumental construction;
- an engineered landscape tied to a floodplain: canal networks, raised fields,
  earthworks.

Range: 10,000 BCE to 1600 CE. Sites carry one of three levels of significance —
capital or metropolis, city or ceremonial centre, village or minor site — which
sets how they are drawn.

Not included: settlements whose water came from a lake, the sea, karst
reservoirs, artificial tanks, wadis, qanats or rainfall alone. Those categories
are set out below, with candidates, in case the map is ever extended. Modern
cities appear only as an optional overlay, to show which of these rivers are
still inhabited.

## Included sites (85 across 40 river systems)

**The Nile** — Memphis (3100 BCE–600 CE), Kerma (2500–1450 BCE), Thebes
(2100–30 BCE), Meroë (800 BCE–350 CE).

**The Limpopo** — Mapungubwe (1075–1220 CE).

(3000–1700 BCE), Nineveh (3000–612 BCE), Mari (2900–1759 BCE), Assur

**The Çarşamba** — Çatalhöyük (7400–6000 BCE).

### Central Asia

**The Helmand** — Mundigak (4000–2200 BCE), Shahr-e Sukhteh (3200–1800 BCE).

### South Asia

## Sites filed loosely

Some entries meet the criteria as cities and towns while sitting well away from the
channel they are filed under. The validator reports any site more than 150 km
from its river's geometry, so these surface on every run.

| Site | Filed under | Distance | Actual water |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Lothal | Indus | 458 km | Sabarmati drainage, Gulf of Khambhat — an Indus civilization port on another river system |
| Marajó | Amazon | 311 km | An island the size of Switzerland at the river's mouth; the mounds are not on the mapped channel |
| Hemudu | Yangtze | 254 km | Coastal wetland on Hangzhou Bay, not the Yangtze proper |
| Dholavira | Indus | 221 km | Two seasonal streams in the Rann of Kachchh, dammed into sixteen reservoirs |
| Pukara | Titicaca | 215 km | The lake's northern shore, not the Desaguadero outlet |
| Angkor | Mekong | 215 km | Tonlé Sap and the Siem Reap river, within the Mekong basin |
| Persepolis | Pulvar & Kor | — | On the Pulvar, but the empire ran on qanats and roads rather than floodplain farming |
| Çatalhöyük | Çarşamba | — | A marshy alluvial fan in a closed basin, not a river bank |

## Lake and inland-sea settlements

Mechanically the closest relatives of river sites: predictable water, fertile
shorelines, fish, and boat transport. Two such site is already on the map — Tiwanaku and
Pukara at Lake Titicaca, where the former is on the Desaguadero River.

| Settlement | Water | Dates | Why it qualifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tenochtitlan | Lake Texcoco | 1325–1521 CE | An island capital fed by *chinampas*, raised garden beds built out into the lake, reached by causeways and an aqueduct from Chapultepec |
| Tušpa (Van) | Lake Van | 830–590 BCE | Urartian capital on a rock above the lake; the Menua canal carried water 56 km and still functions |
| Cuicuilco | Lake Xochimilco, Basin of Mexico | 800–100 BCE | Lakeshore centre with a circular pyramid, buried by the Xitle eruption |
| Sao towns | Lake Chad | 500 BCE–1500 CE | Walled clay towns on the lake's floodplain, known for terracotta figures |
| Shedet (Crocodilopolis) | Lake Moeris, Fayum | 2000 BCE–400 CE | A Nile-fed lake basin regulated by Middle Kingdom hydraulic works on the Bahr Yussef |
| Hasanlu | Lake Urmia basin | 1450–800 BCE | Fortified mound on the Gadar, farming the lake plain |
| Alpine pile dwellings | Alpine lakes | 5000–500 BCE | Hundreds of lakeshore and bog villages on stilts across six countries |
| Biskupin | Lake Biskupińskie | 750–400 BCE | Fortified Iron Age settlement on a lake peninsula, timber ramparts intact |
| Angkor | Tonlé Sap | 800–1431 CE | Already on the map under the Mekong; its reservoirs buffered the lake's annual reversal |

## Sea and ocean settlements

A different mechanism: connectivity and marine protein rather than silt and
irrigation. These are the strongest cases for civilizations that did not need a
river, and would be better as a separate layer than mixed into the river set.

| Settlement | Water | Dates | Why it qualifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knossos and Minoan Crete | Aegean | 1900–1450 BCE | A palace civilization with no significant river, its surplus and reach seaborne |
| Mycenae | Aegean, via the Argolid | 1600–1100 BCE | Hilltop citadel on seasonal streams; grain and metals arrived by ship |
| Byblos, Tyre, Sidon | Mediterranean | 3000–332 BCE | Phoenician ports living on timber, dye and colonies; no river worth the name |
| Carthage | Mediterranean | 814–146 BCE | A harbour city that fed itself on North African and Sicilian grain by sea |
| Athens | Aegean, via Piraeus | 800–338 BCE | Two seasonal streams inland; the city depended on Black Sea grain convoys |
| Dilmun (Bahrain) | Persian Gulf | 2200–1600 BCE | Entrepôt between Mesopotamia and the Indus, watered by artesian springs |
| Nuragic Sardinia | Mediterranean | 1800–500 BCE | Thousands of stone towers on an island economy |
| Kilwa Kisiwani and the Swahili coast | Indian Ocean | 900–1500 CE | Coral-stone cities living on monsoon trade between Africa, Arabia and India |
| Rapa Nui and Polynesia | Pacific | 1200–1600 CE | Monumental building on islands with no rivers at all |
| Norte Chico | Pacific and the Supe valley | 3000–1800 BCE | On the map as a river case, but the Humboldt Current's anchovy fishery supplied the protein; Áspero was the fishing town that fed inland Caral |

## Other water strategies

Neither river, lake nor sea. Worth a layer of their own, since several are among
the most sophisticated water engineering anywhere.

| Settlement | Strategy | Dates |
|---|---|---|
| Chichén Itzá | Cenotes — natural sinkholes into the karst water table | 600–1200 CE |
| Tikal, Calakmul, Caracol | Plastered reservoirs and *aguadas* catching the wet season | 400 BCE–900 CE |
| Anuradhapura, Sigiriya | Enormous artificial tanks and canals | 400 BCE–1000 CE |
| Marib (Saba) | A dam across the Wadi Dhana, irrigating 9,000 hectares | 800 BCE–570 CE |
| Petra | Cisterns, dams and channels capturing flash floods | 300 BCE–100 CE |
| Turpan | *Karez* — underground channels tapping mountain groundwater | 100 BCE onward |
| Aksum | Rainfall and highland terracing alone | 100–700 CE |
| Hattusa | Springs, cisterns and rain-fed uplands | 1650–1200 BCE |
| Great Zimbabwe | Rain-fed plateau grazing and farming | 1100–1450 CE |
| Chaco Canyon, Mesa Verde | Dry farming, seeps, check dams, an intermittent wash | 600–1300 CE |
| Steppe pastoralists | Mobile herding; no permanent settlement | 3300 BCE onward |


## Limitations

- **Sixteen river courses are hand-digitised and approximate**, because Natural
  Earth does not carry them at 50m resolution: the four Peruvian coastal valley
  groups, the Urubamba, the Tiber, the Karkheh and Karun, the Ghaggar–Hakra, the
  Coatzacoalcos and Grijalva, the Río San Juan at Teotihuacan, the Mosna, the
  Atoyac, the Río Grande de Nasca, the Musi, the Çarşamba, the Pulvar, the
  Colorado San Juan and Animas, and the Bolivian Desaguadero. They are accurate
  enough to show the relationship between a city and its water, not to measure.
- **Coastlines are simplified** for file size and look blocky at deep zoom.
- **Significance tiers are editorial.** 38 of 85 sites are marked capital or
  metropolis, which is arguably too many.
- **Coverage follows scholarship and language.** Sub-Saharan Africa (3 sites),
  Southeast Asia (5) and Central Asia (4) are thin because of excavation history
  and English-language sources, not because less happened there.
- **Some entries are landscapes rather than cities** These include Llanos de Moxos,
  Kuhikugu, San Agustín, Newark Earthworks.
- **Population figures are estimates**, marked `est.`, with contested ones
  labelled. Dates are the span the map shows, not claims about precise founding
  years.
- **Europe holds three sites**, which reflects the criteria rather than an
  oversight: European urbanism is largely Mediterranean and maritime.
